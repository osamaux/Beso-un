Section 1: Optical Methods

Microscopy & Examination Techniques in Microbiology

Because microorganisms are invisible to the naked eye, microscopy is fundamental to microbiology. Understanding cell structure requires specialized optical tools ranging from standard light microscopy to high-resolution electron microscopy and atomic force microscopy.

Bright-Field Microscope

The standard compound microscope. The specimen is visualized because of differences in contrast between the organism and the surrounding medium. Dyes and stains are typically required to visualize bacterial morphology.

Resolution: ~0.2 µm
Max Magnification: 1000x - 2000x
Primary Use: Stained bacteria, fungi, parasites

Dark-Field Microscope

Utilizes a special dark-field condenser that blocks direct light. Only light scattered by the specimen enters the objective lens, producing a bright image on a dark background.

Key Advantage: Live unstained cells
Clinical Application: Detection of Treponema pallidum

Phase-Contrast Microscope

Capitalizes on differences in refractive index between cellular structures and water. Converts subtle phase differences in light rays into visible amplitude changes (brightness differences).

Key Advantage: Internal structure of living cells
Features Observed: Endospores, inclusions, vacuoles

Fluorescence Microscope

Exposes specimens to high-energy ultraviolet light, causing fluorochromes (like auramine O or fluorescein) to emit visible light. Used heavily in immunofluorescence.

Clinical Use: Rapid detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis

Confocal Laser Microscope

Uses a focused laser beam and pinhole aperture to scan thin optical sections of thick specimens, eliminating out-of-focus blur. Reconstructs sharp 3D images of complex microbial communities like biofilms.

Key Advantage: 3D optical sectioning

Electron Microscopy (TEM & SEM)

Replaces light with electron beams (wavelength ~0.005 nm). Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) views ultra-thin internal sections. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) renders 3D surface topography.

Resolution TEM: ~0.001 µm (0.1 nm)
Primary Use: Viruses, cell ultrastructure
Section 2: Cellular Architecture

Eukaryotic vs Prokaryotic Cell Organization

All living cells are classified as either eukaryotic or prokaryotic. Bacteria and Archaea are prokaryotes, while fungi, protozoa, plants, and animals are eukaryotes.

Structural & Biochemical Comparison

Characteristic Prokaryotic Cells (Bacteria) Eukaryotic Cells (Fungi, Human)
Nucleus & Membrane No nuclear membrane; single circular nucleoid. True membrane-bound nucleus with nucleolus & linear chromosomes.
Membrane-Bound Organelles Absent (No mitochondria, ER, or Golgi). Present (Mitochondria, ER, Golgi apparatus, Lysosomes).
Ribosome Size 70S Ribosomes (30S + 50S subunits). 80S Ribosomes (40S + 60S subunits in cytoplasm).
Cell Wall Chemical Composition Contains peptidoglycan (except Mycoplasmas & Archaea). No peptidoglycan; contains cellulose (plants) or chitin (fungi).
Plasma Membrane Lipids Lacks sterols (except Mycoplasma species). Contains sterols (cholesterol in animal cells, ergosterol in fungi).
Locomotion Structures Submicroscopic flagella made of flagellin (rotates like a propeller). Complex cilia and flagella containing 9+2 microtubule pattern.
Section 3: Prokaryotic Cell Envelope

The Cell Wall & Plasma Membrane Structure

The bacterial cell envelope consists of the plasma membrane, the cell wall, and in Gram-negative bacteria, an outer membrane.

Gram-Positive Cell Wall

Stains Purple

Consists of a thick, multi-layered peptidoglycan mesh (up to 80 nm thick) outside the plasma membrane.

Key Chemical Components:

  • Peptidoglycan (Murein): Polymers of alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM).
  • Teichoic Acid: Polyol phosphate polymers covalently linked to peptidoglycan; acts as major surface antigens.
  • Lipoteichoic Acid (LTA): Teichoic acids anchored directly into the glycolipids of the underlying cytoplasmic membrane; triggers host inflammatory responses.

Gram-Negative Cell Wall

Stains Pink/Red

Consists of a thin peptidoglycan layer (1-3 nm) surrounded by a complex asymmetric outer membrane separated by the periplasmic space.

Key Chemical Components:

  • Lipopolysaccharide (LPS / Endotoxin): Located in outer leaflet of outer membrane. Contains Lipid A (toxic anchor), Core Polysaccharide, and O-Antigen repeats.
  • Porins: Transmembrane protein channels that permit passive diffusion of hydrophilic molecules (sugars, amino acids) into the periplasm.
  • Periplasmic Enzymes: Contains β-lactamases that destroy penicillin and cephalosporins.

Acid-Fast Cell Wall (Mycobacteria)

Contains a rich waxy outer layer composed of mycolic acids, arabinogalactan, and waxes. Makes Mycobacterium species extremely resistant to drying, chemicals, and standard Gram staining (requires Acid-Fast staining).

Cell Wall-Deficient Bacteria & L-Forms

Mycoplasma species naturally lack a cell wall and contain sterols in their membrane. L-forms are cell wall-deficient mutants produced in laboratory conditions or antibiotic exposure (protoplasts and spheroplasts).

Section 4: Internal Structures & Surface Extras

Prokaryotic Cytoplasm & Surface Appendages

Despite their simple organization, prokaryotic cells possess specialized internal inclusions, nucleoid DNA, plasmids, and external structures like flagella, pili, and endospores.

Bacterial Flagella

Helical appendages composed of flagellin protein attached to a basal body that acts as a rotary motor. Enables chemotaxis.

• Monotrichous: Single polar flagellum
• Lophotrichous: Tuft of flagella at one pole
• Peritrichous: Flagella all over cell surface

Pili & Fimbriae

Hair-like appendages made of pilin subunit proteins.

  • • Ordinary Pili (Fimbriae): Mediate adherence to host cell receptors (colonization factor).
  • • Sex Pili (F-Pili): Form conjugation tubes for horizontal transfer of plasmids between bacteria.

Bacterial Endospores

Dormant, highly resistant structures produced by Bacillus and Clostridium species during nutrient starvation.

Heat Resistance Key: High levels of calcium dipicolinate (dipicolinic acid) and dehydrated core.

Capsule & Slime Layer

Viscous polysaccharide layer outside the cell wall. Protects pathogenic bacteria against phagocytosis by host immune cells (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae).

Inclusion Bodies & PHB

Intracellular nutrient reserves. Include Poly-β-hydroxybutyric acid (PHB) granules, polyphosphate (volutin/metachromatic granules in Corynebacterium), and glycogen.

Magnetosomes & Gas Vesicles

Magnetosomes contain magnetite crystals that guide aquatic bacteria down toward sediments. Gas vesicles provide buoyancy in aquatic photosynthetic species.

Section 5: Microbiological Staining Procedures

Gram Stain & Diagnostic Staining Techniques

Because bacteria are virtually transparent in aqueous suspension, staining is crucial for morphological classification. The Gram stain developed by Christian Gram in 1884 remains the most important differential stain in clinical microbiology.

The Gram Stain Procedure & Mechanism

1 Primary Stain

Crystal Violet

Applied for 1 minute. Stains all bacterial cells a deep purple color by penetrating peptidoglycan.

2 Mordant

Gram's Iodine

Applied for 1 minute. Forms a large insoluble CV-Iodine complex inside peptidoglycan.

3 Decolorizer

95% Ethanol / Acetone

Critical step (10-15 sec). Dissolves lipid outer membrane in Gram-negatives and washes out CV-I. Gram-positives retain CV-I due to dehydrated thick mesh.

4 Counterstain

Safranin

Applied for 1 minute. Stains decolorized Gram-negative cells pink/red, while Gram-positive cells remain purple.

Acid-Fast Stain (Ziehl-Neelsen)

Uses carbolfuchsin with heat to penetrate waxy mycolic acid walls. Resists decolorization with acid-alcohol. Acid-fast organisms stain bright red; background stains blue with methylene blue.

Endospore Stain (Schaeffer-Fulton)

Uses malachite green driven into spores with heat/steam. Vegetative cells are counterstained with safranin. Spores appear green; vegetative cells appear pink.

Capsule Stain (Negative Staining)

Uses India Ink or nigrosin. Background becomes dark gray/black, while capsules stand out as clear unstained halos surrounding colored bacterial bodies.
